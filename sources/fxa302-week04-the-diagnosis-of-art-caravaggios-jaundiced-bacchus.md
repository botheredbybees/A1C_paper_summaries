## The diagnosis of art: Caravaggio's jaundiced Bacchus

This short clinical-humanities note examines Caravaggio's early self-portrait *Young Sick Bacchus* (*Bacchino malato*, c. 1593-94), painted in the two years after the artist fell ill in his first months in Rome and spent six months in the hospital of Santa Maria della Consolazione. Aronson and Ramachandran (2007) argue that the painting is not simply an allegorical subversion of the wine god's usual vigour but a documentary record of Caravaggio's own illness: the flesh tones match the pallor of the peaches on the table before him, and the sclerae of the eyes show a distinct yellow tinge, both consistent with jaundice. They support this reading by comparing the painting with a second, markedly healthier Bacchus that Caravaggio painted around 1596, once he had recovered. The authors suggest Caravaggio would have observed jaundice first-hand in chronically ill alcoholics dying of liver failure in the hospital, giving him a visual vocabulary he then turned on himself while recovering from an acute (rather than chronic) illness. The piece sits alongside this week's other retrospective-diagnosis readings (Bianucci et al., 2018, on breast cancer in Renaissance portraiture; Asensi et al., 2019, on hypothyroidism in a Lorenzo Lotto portrait) as an example of naturalistic Renaissance and early Baroque painting being read by clinicians as physical evidence of disease.
