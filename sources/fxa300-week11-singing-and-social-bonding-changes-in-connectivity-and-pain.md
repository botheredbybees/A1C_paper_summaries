## Singing and social bonding: changes in connectivity and pain threshold as a function of group size

The study explores the impact of group singing on social bonding and endorphin release in varying group sizes, aiming to understand if singing can facilitate social cohesion in large groups, a key evolutionary challenge for humans. Participants from a community choir engaged in singing sessions in both small (20-80 individuals) and large (232 individuals) groups. Measures of social bonding and pain thresholds, a proxy for endorphin release, were taken before and after the sessions. The results indicated that singing increased feelings of inclusion, connectivity, positive affect, and endorphin release in both small and large groups. However, greater changes in social closeness were observed in the large group context. This suggests that singing may serve as an efficient mechanism for social bonding, particularly in larger groups, aligning with evolutionary theories positing music as a means to manage social cohesion beyond the limitations of one-on-one grooming observed in non-human primates. The findings highlight the role of music in fostering social connections and enhancing well-being in large social gatherings, which is crucial for understanding human social evolution.
