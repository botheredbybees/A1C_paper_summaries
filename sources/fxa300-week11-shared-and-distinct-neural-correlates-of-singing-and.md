## Shared and distinct neural correlates of singing and speaking

This study, conducted by Elif Özdemir, Andrea Norton, and Gottfried Schlaug, explores the neural correlates of singing and speaking using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) on 10 right-handed participants. The research aimed to identify both shared and distinct neural substrates involved in these vocal activities. The participants were asked to repeat intoned (“sung”) and non-intoned (“spoken”) bisyllabic words/phrases, and these tasks were contrasted with conditions controlling for pitch (“humming”) and basic motor processes related to vocalization (“vowel production”). The study revealed significant overlap in neural activity between singing and speaking, particularly in the inferior pre- and post-central gyri, superior temporal gyrus (STG), and superior temporal sulcus (STS) bilaterally. These areas are crucial for motor preparation and execution, as well as sensory feedback and control for vocal production. However, differences were observed between speaking and singing, with speaking showing additional activation in the inferior frontal gyrus and primary sensorimotor cortex, while singing showed additional activation in the STG and the primary sensorimotor cortex. The findings suggest a bihemispheric network for vocal production, regardless of whether the words/phrases are intoned or spoken. Additionally, the study observed right-lateralized activation in the superior temporal gyrus, inferior central operculum, and inferior frontal gyrus when comparing singing to humming, which may explain why patients with left hemisphere lesions can sing lyrics while unable to speak them. This research provides valuable insights into the neural mechanisms underlying singing and speaking, contributing to our understanding of the interplay between music and language in the brain.
