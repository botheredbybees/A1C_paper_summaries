## Week 3 Lecture: Brain Development and Cognitive Reserve

Dr Brendan Lamb's lecture explores the relationship between creativity and brain development in adolescence and early childhood. The presentation begins with an overview of brain anatomy, including the functions of different lobes such as the frontal lobe for higher-order functions, the parietal lobe for sensory integration, the occipital lobe for visual processing, and the temporal lobe for auditory processing and memory. Additionally, synesthesia is discussed as a neurological phenomenon involving the blending of senses.

The lecture delves into the concept of the Default Mode Network (DMN), which operates spontaneously without goal-directed tasks. It also covers grey matter and white matter in brain development, emphasizing that early life experiences can significantly impact neural connections through proliferation and selective pruning. Research indicates a critical role for creativity training during adolescence to support healthy brain development.

The lecture further discusses how stress impacts brain architecture, with moderate stress aiding normal coping mechanisms when experienced in nurturing environments. However, toxic stress can reorganize brain structure, affecting executive functions like planning and self-regulation. The Cognitive Reserve Theory is introduced as a concept that explains the brain's ability to compensate for cell deterioration through alternative pathways developed from education, occupation, leisure activities, and other environmental factors.

The lecture concludes with insights into the benefits of music in childhood, particularly the controversial but intriguing 'Mozart Effect,' which suggests temporary cognitive improvements from listening to classical music. It also addresses empirical evidence supporting specific cognitive enhancements such as memory improvement and mental rotation skills resulting from deliberate engagement with music education.
