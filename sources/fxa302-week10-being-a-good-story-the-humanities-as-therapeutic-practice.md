## Being a Good Story: The Humanities as Therapeutic Practice

Arthur W. Frank's chapter explores the role of the humanities in helping individuals navigate illness through storytelling and narrative creation. Frank introduces the concept by recalling a conversation with a prostate cancer patient who questioned the value of discussing illness when faced with constant pain. This encounter highlights the challenge of advising someone who is deeply affected by their illness while maintaining empathy and avoiding pretentiousness. Frank suggests that the humanities offer resources to help ill people construct narratives that bring interest, value, and pleasure to their lives, countering the often dehumanizing nature of medical care. He outlines four distinctions that are crucial for understanding the role of the humanities in illness: the difference between illness and disease, being a patient versus being an ill person, the distinction between a medical history and a personal story, and the contrast between treatment and care. These distinctions emphasize the importance of storytelling as a means to maintain a sense of self and find meaning in the face of illness. Frank argues that narrative can serve as a therapeutic tool, enabling individuals to rediscover interest in life and connect with others beyond the confines of their medical condition.
