## Depicting Blindness in Medieval and Renaissance Art

This lesson explores how blindness has been depicted in medieval and renaissance art. It begins with the scarcity of visual images from the mediaeval period that depict blindness but those present are religious allegories or stories of healing miracles such as Jesus restoring sight to the blind. A notable example is an illumination from a 14th-century manuscript depicting a blind man being led by a dog, symbolizing guidance and charity towards the disabled. The Renaissance era saw Pieter Breughel the Elder's work featuring blind villagers, emphasizing their struggle against faith, while Caravaggio's dramatic depiction of Saul's conversion on the road to Damascus highlights the emotional impact of blindness as an allegory for religious transformation. Seventeenth-century Baroque art often focused on strong human emotion and drama in biblical narratives involving blindness or its cure, with a notable example being Bernardo Strozzi’s portrayal of Tobit curing his father's blindness using fish bile. The lesson concludes by revisiting the literary depiction of blindness through John Milton's sonnet 'When I consider how my light is spent', illustrating the impact of blindness on creativity.
