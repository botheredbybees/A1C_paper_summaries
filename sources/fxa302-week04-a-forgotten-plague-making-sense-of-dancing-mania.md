## A Forgotten Plague: Making Sense of Dancing Mania

This article explores the phenomenon of medieval 'dancing mania,' a series of outbreaks where individuals danced uncontrollably, often for days or weeks at a time. Waller presents evidence from historical records suggesting that these events were not isolated incidents but rather widespread phenomena occurring primarily along the Rhine and Moselle rivers in central Europe during the 14th to 16th centuries. He argues against theories attributing dancing mania to ergot poisoning, instead positing that it was a psychological reaction exacerbated by severe environmental conditions and social stressors. The author draws parallels with modern anthropological studies of possession rituals, suggesting that pre-existing cultural beliefs about divine curses or punishments may have triggered these events. Waller also highlights the role of religious practices in both perpetuating and eventually mitigating the outbreaks through exorcism rituals and pilgrimage to shrines dedicated to saints associated with such plagues.
