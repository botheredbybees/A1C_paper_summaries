## Why the brain is programmed to see faces in everyday objects

The phenomenon of seeing faces in non-human objects, known as face pareidolia, is a common occurrence that reflects how our brains are wired. A study by Dr Colin Palmer from the University of New South Wales (UNSW) shows that the brain processes these perceived 'fake' faces using similar mechanisms to those used for real human faces. The research highlights that even objects with minimal facial features can trigger perceptions of gaze direction and emotional expression, suggesting an evolutionary advantage in being overly sensitive to detecting faces. This sensitivity might help humans detect social cues efficiently but also leads to false positives where non-human objects are perceived as having human-like characteristics. Understanding this process could provide insights into cognitive disorders related to face recognition and social interaction difficulties.
