## Cinematic Representation of Locked-In Syndrome

The provided sources examine the cinematic portrayal of locked-in syndrome, focusing on Julian Schnabel's adaptation of 'The Diving Bell and the Butterfly.' Both sources emphasize the unique challenges of representing a condition that severely limits physical interaction with the world. The concept of disembodied consciousness is highlighted, with the film's narrative style emphasizing the subjective camera, which mirrors the protagonist's limited perspective. The notion of cinematic spectatorship is critiqued, suggesting that the film invites viewers into an intimate and isolating experience that diverges from conventional narrative structures. The social model of disability is also considered, questioning how the film addresses or challenges societal perceptions of disability and consciousness. These explorations contribute to a theoretical framework around how cinema can represent and communicate extreme conditions of human experience, particularly focusing on the interplay between the subjective and the social.

## Key Findings

- The film 'The Diving Bell and the Butterfly' uses subjective camera techniques to immerse the viewer in the protagonist's limited perspective.
- Cinematic representation of locked-in syndrome challenges conventional notions of cinematic spectatorship, offering a deeply personal and isolating viewing experience.
- The social model of disability is implicitly addressed through the film's narrative, prompting reflection on societal perceptions of consciousness and disability.

## Open Questions

- How do subjective camera techniques used in 'The Diving Bell and the Butterfly' influence public understanding and empathy towards individuals with locked-in syndrome?
