## Medieval Plagues and Social Upheavals

The study of medieval plagues, such as the Black Death and Dancing Mania, provides a critical lens into the complex interplay between disease, societal structures, and human behavior. Source 1 delves into the phenomenon of Dancing Mania, an unexplained outbreak of mass hysteria in medieval Europe that manifested through uncontrollable dancing. One prominent theory posited is ergot poisoning from rye bread contaminated with Claviceps purpurea fungus. However, the influence of exorcism rituals and societal anxieties cannot be disregarded, suggesting a multifaceted causality involving both biological and psychological factors. In contrast, Source 2 focuses on the Black Death, caused by Yersinia pestis bacterium, emphasizing its significant demographic shifts and social upheavals in medieval Europe. The empirical approach to medicine during this period reveals limited understanding of disease vectors but notable efforts to manage the spread through quarantine measures. Both sources highlight the profound impact of these plagues not only on mortality rates but also on societal norms, religion, and economic structures, illustrating a paradigm where natural disasters intertwined with human responses in shaping history.

## Key Findings

- Both Dancing Mania and the Black Death had significant impacts on medieval European societies beyond their immediate biological effects.
- Theories about causation of medieval plagues range from microbial to psychological origins.
- Empirical medicine during this period was limited but showed attempts at disease control through quarantine.

## Open Questions

- How did the combined influence of ergot poisoning and societal factors contribute to the unique manifestation of Dancing Mania?
