## Visual Culture and Disability Representation

The intersection of visual culture and disability studies is explored through two distinct yet interconnected lenses. Source 1 delves into re-enactment phototherapy, a therapeutic intervention that uses photography to facilitate embodiment and transformation in individuals, often addressing the psychological impact of how one is perceived visually by others, incorporating concepts from gaze theory. This source highlights the role of visual representation in personal identity formation and healing processes. In contrast, Source 2 examines the broader cultural context of disability through art history, focusing on the iconography of blindness and its portrayal across different time periods and artistic movements. It discusses how artists have represented blind individuals, reflecting societal attitudes towards disability. Both sources underscore the significance of visual representation in shaping public perceptions and personal identities within the realm of disability studies.

## Key Findings

- Photography can serve as a therapeutic tool for individuals to explore their visual identity and embodied experiences.
- Historical art representations influence contemporary understanding and portrayal of disabilities such as blindness.
- Visual culture plays a critical role in both shaping societal attitudes towards disability and facilitating personal transformation.

## Open Questions

- How do modern therapeutic practices like re-enactment phototherapy interact with historical visual portrayals of disability to affect current perceptions?
