## General Theory Of Verbal Humor

The General Theory of Verbal Humor (GTVH) is a comprehensive theoretical framework that seeks to explain the mechanisms underlying verbal humor. GTVH posits that successful verbal humor relies on breaking expectations, often through incongruity or violation of script oppositions. Across various studies and contexts—ranging from stand-up comedy to political speechmaking—the theory demonstrates its versatility in explaining different forms of humor such as self-deprecating jokes, metaphorical humor, and deliberate metaphors used for humorous effect. Additionally, GTVH has been applied to analyze the distribution patterns of humor within literary texts and television content like satirical news shows. While these applications highlight the robustness of GTVH, there are also critiques regarding its reliance on specific conversational principles and cognitive complexity, suggesting areas where further refinement might be necessary.

## Key Findings

- The General Theory of Verbal Humor (GTVH) provides a foundational explanation for how verbal humor works by focusing on the violation of expectations.
- Across various contexts such as comedy performances, political speeches, and literary analysis, GTVH proves its applicability in understanding diverse forms of verbal humor.
- Critiques suggest that while GTVH is robust, it may benefit from considering additional conversational principles beyond those originally proposed.

## Open Questions

- How can the General Theory of Verbal Humor be further refined to account for different cultural and linguistic contexts in which humor operates?
