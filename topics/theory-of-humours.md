## Theory Of Humours

The theory of humours, an ancient Greek concept, posits that the human body is governed by four bodily fluids or humours: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile. These fluids are thought to influence personality and physical health. Source 1 discusses the case of Homer, the ancient Greek poet, and the attribution of his blindness to either divine retribution or natural causes, highlighting the theory of humours as a framework for understanding health and disease. Source 2 delves into melancholia, a condition associated with an excess of black bile, and links it to creativity as a form of emotional regulation. Both sources underscore the theory of humours as a foundational concept in ancient medicine and psychology, though its relevance in modern scientific contexts is limited.

## Key Findings

- The theory of humours was used to explain both physical and mental conditions in ancient Greece.
- Figurative blindness in Homer may symbolize a deeper insight or wisdom rather than a physical disability.
- Melancholia, linked to an imbalance of the humours, is associated with heightened creativity and emotional processing.

## Open Questions

- How do modern psychological theories and practices build upon or contrast with the ancient concept of humours in understanding mental health?
