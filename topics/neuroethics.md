## Neuroethics in Brain-Computer Interfaces and Personhood

Neuroethics emerges as a critical discourse in the context of brain-computer interface (BCI) technologies and their implications for personhood and disability. Across the provided sources, there is a consistent focus on ethical considerations in the application of neural technologies, particularly in scenarios such as locked-in syndrome (LIS). Source 1 delves into the concept of personhood from both the third and first-person perspectives, emphasizing the importance of self-identity and interpersonal recognition in defining a person's status despite severe physical disabilities. Source 2 examines the practical and ethical dimensions of expanding sensory capabilities through neurotechnology, highlighting the potential of neural plasticity and speech decoding to enhance quality of life for individuals with LIS. However, Source 3 cautions about the responsibilities and ethical dilemmas surrounding the use of BCIs, particularly the risk of ignoring or misinterpreting the communication attempts of LIS patients, which can impact their human rights and quality of life. The integration of these perspectives underscores the multifaceted nature of neuroethics, encompassing technical, social, and ethical aspects that must be carefully navigated to ensure the ethical use of BCIs and the preservation of personhood.

## Key Findings

- Neuroethics is crucial in the ethical application of brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) to address the needs of individuals with locked-in syndrome (LIS).
- The concept of personhood is challenged and redefined in the context of LIS, emphasizing the importance of self-identity and interpersonal recognition.
- Neural plasticity and neurofeedback therapy offer potential to improve the quality of life for individuals with LIS, yet these advances must be balanced with ethical considerations.

## Open Questions

- How can ethical frameworks be developed to ensure the responsible use of brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) in enhancing the quality of life for individuals with locked-in syndrome (LIS) while preserving their human rights and personhood?
