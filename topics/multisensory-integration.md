## Multisensory Integration

The concept of multisensory integration is explored through the use of sensory substitution devices that aid individuals with auditory and pitch perception deficits. Source 1 examines an audio-tactile device's impact on speech reception in noisy environments, highlighting the principle that optimal performance occurs when stimuli intensity surpasses a threshold but declines as it increases further (Inverse Effectiveness Principle). In contrast, source 2 focuses on vibrotactile gloves aiding hard-of-hearing individuals' pitch perception and melodic reproduction, emphasizing sensory substitution rather than direct auditory enhancement. These approaches are underpinned by neurological studies in Source 3, which uses diffusion-tensor-imaging to identify anatomical connections between auditory and tactile systems that support cross-modal interactions. The integration of these techniques underscores the potential for non-traditional sensory inputs to enhance or replace deficient modalities, particularly in scenarios where conventional treatments may be insufficient.

## Key Findings

- Multisensory integration devices can improve speech reception thresholds in noisy environments by compensating for auditory deficits with tactile stimuli.
- Vibrotactile feedback enhances pitch perception and melodic reproduction abilities among hard-of-hearing individuals, highlighting the efficacy of sensory substitution technologies.
- Neurological studies reveal anatomical pathways that support cross-modal interactions between auditory and tactile systems, providing a biological basis for multisensory integration interventions.

## Open Questions

- How do individual differences in neuroplasticity influence the effectiveness and long-term sustainability of sensory substitution devices?
